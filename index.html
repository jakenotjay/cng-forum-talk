From Days to Minutes: A Just-in-Time Pipeline for Plot-Level Supply-Chain Analytics

Jake Wilkins

Mapping & quantifying the first mile

At Epoch we’re mapping and quantifying the first mile of soft commodity supply chains, things like palm oil, cocoa and soy. Our customers are mostly managing risk in those supply chains, both regulatory, like the EUDR, and longer-term degradation from land use change.

Supply chains remain obscure

Through a long and complicated history, most companies have very little idea where their raw commodities actually come from. There are often five or more tiers between a brand and the farm, and most have no visibility past the first one.

TRACE: from the brand down to the facility

TRACE does that tracing for them. We start from the CPG company and work our way down the tiers, which can run six or seven deep, aggregating web sources, media and proprietary sources, until we get to the facilities actually sourcing the commodity.

Addresses in, plot-level risk out

From there it’s addresses in and plot-level risk out. We geocode and verify each facility, generate a realistic supply shed from the road network, terrain and land cover, delineate every commodity plot inside it, and then compute environmental metrics for all of them.

Ninety minutes by road from the mill

For each mill we turn the Overture road network, a global elevation model and land cover into a travel-time surface, and the supply shed is everywhere a truck can reach within ninety minutes, so it follows the roads.

One global array, filled as we go

If you were at CNG London you might remember this. Every dataset is one global icechunk array, filled incrementally: a new supply shed checks which chunks already exist, fills the missing ones, and commits the data and metaarray in one transaction.

Computed on demand, per supply shed

All of this is computed on the fly, as a customer requests it. Each supply shed goes through a series of Prefect jobs on Kubernetes: export to icechunk, zonal stats, then normalise into Postgres. Today I’m mostly talking about that first step.

Earth Engine: 500 requests, 4 facilities

  • unexplained costs
  • unstable costs
  • no visibility into processing
  • not enough throughput

The problem was what was filling those arrays: Earth Engine. The high volume endpoint limits you to 500 concurrent requests, which for us meant four facilities at a time, fully saturated, with costs we couldn’t really explain or predict.

The solution: VirtualiZarr

So the solution: the cube! Or more specifically, the virtual cube. With VirtualiZarr we read the source files directly, and the only change is that the references live in our bucket on GCS while the bytes stay wherever the provider put them.

Of course, nearly all of it is TIFFs, which brings a few wrinkles. People like to write their tiffs on UTM grids, or on global ten-degree tiles, or they overwrite them in place whenever they fancy, which the alerts datasets like GLAD do every single day.

Ragged tiles ≠ one array

#1111 Concatenate arrays with padded final chunks

The ten-degree tiles are the sneaky one. The tile size rarely divides evenly by the block size, so every tile ends in a smaller chunk, and stitching them gives you irregular chunks. I raised it on VirtualiZarr, and sadly it’s quite hard to fix.

So instead we split our datasets into three families: static global manifests that open as one stitched array, tiled static manifests like TMF, and dynamic stores virtualised on the fly. Whichever family, it all ends up reprojected onto the target geobox.

In code, every family implements one protocol: open takes a target geobox and a date window, and returns a lazy xarray dataset on our global grid. That means the rest of the pipeline never needs to know which family it’s talking to.

Earth Engine spend, month by month

This is our Earth Engine spend by month. The June bump was thumbnails and isochrones, which we’ve since moved off it, and once the xarray lane took over in July the bill fell to about three per cent of last winter’s.

What we gained

2× quicker per job, same compute

64 concurrent jobs up from 4

Stable under load

Leaving Earth Engine paid off well beyond the bill. Any given job now takes about half the time on the same compute, we’ve load tested up to sixty-four concurrent jobs, up from four, and we’re far more stable under load.
